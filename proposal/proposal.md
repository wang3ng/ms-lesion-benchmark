# Project Full Proposal

**Benchmarking nnU-Net against MedSAM for Multiple Sclerosis Lesion Segmentation**

This draft is organized under the four proposal criteria. The week-by-week schedule, experiment IDs, and exit tests remain in [`DEVELOPMENT_PLAN.md`](../DEVELOPMENT_PLAN.md).

## Problem Area

Multiple sclerosis is monitored with MRI, and the clinically useful numbers are how many white-matter lesions a patient has and how much volume they occupy. Those numbers still come mostly from manual outlines. Manual work is slow, and even trained readers disagree: on the MSSEG-2016 cohort, seven experts marked the same scans and the best and worst experts were far apart on both overlap and lesion detection. Automatic segmentation is therefore worth studying, but the usual score, voxel-wise Dice, answers the wrong question for this disease. A large lesion can keep Dice high while the model misses the tiny, scattered lesions that change the count and barely change the volume. A second failure is cross-scanner generalization. The same nominal protocol on Siemens, Philips, and GE hardware does not look the same to a network, so a model that looks strong on one site can fail on another. Both failures matter for research and for care: lesion counts and volumes are used to judge whether disease is active, and multi-center trials only work if the measurement survives a change of scanner. This project treats those two gaps, small-lesion detection and unseen-scanner robustness, as the problem to measure, and then checks whether the resulting masks still reproduce manual lesion count and volume.

## Context

Prior MS lesion challenges showed that automatic methods were not yet at expert level. In the MICCAI 2016 MSSEG analysis, thirteen pipelines, including early machine-learning and deep-learning entries, were scored against a seven-expert consensus; the best automatic method still trailed the best expert on both delineation and lesion detection (segmentation scores on the order of 0.59 versus about 0.78 for the best expert, and detection scores on the order of 0.49 versus about 0.89), and performance dropped further on images from a scanner withheld from training (Commowick et al., 2018). Since then, nnU-Net (Isensee et al., 2021) became the specialist baseline: a self-configuring convolutional network that sets its own preprocessing, patch size, and architecture from the data and has been the default strong result across biomedical segmentation tasks. On the foundation-model side, the Segment Anything review is the relevant lesson. SAM (Kirillov et al., 2023) is a promptable segmenter with a heavy image encoder, a fast prompt encoder, and a light mask decoder, trained with more than a billion masks on 11 million images. In that paper, zero-shot SAM often matched or beat supervised methods across 23 datasets. The same review also recorded the limits that matter here: SAM misses fine structure, adds small disconnected pieces, and loses to domain-specific tools inside a niche. MedSAM (Ma et al., 2024) moves that idea into medicine, with about 1.6 million image–mask pairs over 10 modalities, and reports higher accuracy than modality-specific specialists on a large internal and external validation suite. It is still a 2D, prompt-driven model, and it was not built to count punctate MS lesions or to be tested on a held-out MRI vendor. A later MS-specific adaptation, MS-SAM-LESS (Proietto Salanitri et al., 2026), uses SAM only while learning dense prompts and replaces it at inference with a small convolutional head, with reported gains on the public MSLesSeg set. What is still missing is a locked comparison of a specialist 3D model (nnU-Net) and a medical foundation model (MedSAM) under lesion-wise detection F1, including tiny lesions, and under the official unseen-scanner split rather than Dice alone.

## Data

All volumes are multi-contrast brain MRI stored as NIfTI, with a binary or consensus lesion mask. No scans are committed to the repository; they are downloaded into a gitignored `data/raw/` directory. The primary set is MSSEG-2016: 53 patients, one exam each, from four scanners and three manufacturers (Siemens Verio 3T, Siemens Aera 1.5T, Philips Ingenia 3T, GE Discovery 3T). The official split is 15 training exams and 38 test exams. GE Discovery is absent from training (0 cases) and contributes 8 of the test exams, which is the cross-scanner test. Each exam includes 3D FLAIR, 3D T1, 3D T1 after gadolinium, and an axial dual-echo proton-density/T2 pair. Seven experts outlined FLAIR-hyperintense lesions; the label we train and score against is the published LOP STAPLE consensus, not one rater. Access is through Shanoir and may be limited to the 15 training cases until the test release is approved. The transfer set is the ISBI 2015 longitudinal challenge: 5 training subjects with a mean of 4.4 timepoints (about 21 volumes) and 14 test subjects with a similar number of timepoints, all from one 3T Philips scanner, with T1-weighted MPRAGE, T2, proton density, and FLAIR, and two independent rater masks. The public fallbacks, used if MSSEG test access is delayed, are MSLesSeg (75 patients, 115 timepoints, T1/T2/FLAIR, of which the published training split is 53 patients; preprocessed zip about 1.37 GB), MS3SEG (100 patients, one Toshiba 1.5T scanner, thick-slice axial T2-FLAIR at about 0.90 × 0.90 × 5.73 mm plus registered T1 and T2, tri-mask labels of which only abnormal white-matter hyperintensity is the lesion class; NIfTI portion about 2 GB), and 3D-MR-MS (30 patients with consensus white-matter lesion masks, six zip archives totaling about 4.5 GB, CC BY-NC-ND, kept local). Together that is on the order of 50 to 250 labeled exams depending on which releases are on disk, spanning 3D research protocols and thick-slice clinical scans.

## Proposed Solution

The study compares two existing algorithms rather than inventing a new network. nnU-Net v2 is the specialist baseline: `3d_fullres` on the volumetric cohorts and `2d` on MS3SEG, whose 5.7 mm slices cannot support a 3D kernel, with the planner’s own preprocessing and patch size left unchanged. MedSAM is the foundation-model arm. Its ViT image encoder stays frozen at first, and the prompt encoder and mask decoder are fine-tuned on the same training cases. Because a hand-drawn box would make MedSAM semi-automatic, the primary condition uses automatic prompts (boxes around a frozen nnU-Net proposal, plus a background point), runs slice by slice, and stacks the slices into a 3D mask before scoring. An oracle-box run, prompted from the reference lesions, is reported only as an upper bound. Evaluation uses one metric implementation for both models: voxel Dice; lesion-wise detection F1 under the MSSEG rule (26-connected components, sufficient overlap, and rejection of a predicted blob that is far larger than the reference lesion); the same F1 restricted to punctate lesions, defined in advance as equivalent-sphere diameter under 3 mm; and agreement of lesion count and lesion volume in millilitres (correlation, ICC, MAE, and Bland–Altman plots). The main problems we expect are practical and scientific. The 38-case MSSEG test set, including all eight GE exams, may stay behind an access request, in which case the scanner claim is reported as blocked and the open datasets still produce the detection and volume results. A 12 GB GPU may force a smaller nnU-Net patch. Automatic MedSAM prompts that come from nnU-Net make a pipeline, not a pure foundation model, so that dependency has to be labeled. Eight GE cases are too few for a precise vendor effect, so every case will be listed. SAM’s known weakness on fine, disconnected structure may show up as a Dice score that looks acceptable beside a poor punctate F1, which is the comparison the study is designed to show.
